# The Bosnian Genocide — A Comparative Analysis with the Circassian Genocide

A comparative study of why one genocide was recognised and prosecuted while another, larger in duration and displacement, was not.

**Authors:** Mamun Haupsh (Senior Researcher, Amman, Jordan) · Naser M. Tahboub (Department of International Relations, Prince Al Hussein Bin Abdullah II School of International Studies, University of Jordan)

**Status:** In press — *Dirasat: Human and Social Sciences*, University of Jordan
**Language:** English, with an Arabic abstract
**Keywords:** genocide · Bosnia · Circassians · international law · ICTY · ICJ · politics of recognition

---

## What this paper argues

Bosnia (1992–1995) and the Circassian expulsions (1860s) were both campaigns of mass killing and forced removal. Only one of them ended in tribunals, judgments and an official name. The paper asks why, and answers that the difference lies less in the atrocities themselves than in what existed around them.

Bosnia happened after the Genocide Convention, and inside a system that had the ICTY and the ICJ to hand. It happened in front of cameras, and at a moment when acting on it suited the interests of states with the power to act. The Circassian case predates that entire legal architecture, and the state responsible — Russia — has retained enough standing since to keep the question closed.

The conclusion the paper draws is uncomfortable and deliberate: recognition tracks timing, institutions, political will and advocacy, not the scale or intent of the killing.

## Method

Comparative qualitative analysis across three bodies of material:

- **Legal instruments and case law** — the Genocide Convention, ICTY and ICJ proceedings, *Jorgić v. Germany* on universal jurisdiction
- **Historical record** — the Circassian expulsions of 1860–1864 and the Bosnian war
- **Secondary literature** — scholarship on recognition politics, diaspora mobilisation and R2P

The analytical frame draws on international legal doctrine and political science accounts of institutions, state interest, media attention and diaspora activism.

## Research questions

1. Why was the genocide against Bosnians recognised and prosecuted while the Circassian genocide was not?
2. What role do law, politics and activism play in whether a genocide is recognised and punished?

## Hypothesis

Political and institutional context shapes international response at least as powerfully as the gravity of the atrocity. Cases taken up by major states or by advocacy movements working through existing legal instruments are far more likely to reach recognition and prosecution than those without that backing.

## Recommendations

- Strengthen the reach and speed of international legal bodies, including the ICC and regional courts, and clarify the triggers for R2P
- Reduce the effect of great-power vetoes on atrocity determinations; establish an independent panel to assess genocide claims on evidence rather than politics
- Support survivor and diaspora communities in archiving oral history, building documentation centres and pursuing legal research
- Extend commemoration and curricula beyond the recognised cases; convene a scholarly commission on the Circassian genocide
- Expand preventive diplomacy, early warning, and the use of universal jurisdiction to close accountability gaps
